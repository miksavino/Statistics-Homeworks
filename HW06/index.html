Independence in Statistics and Probability

In data analysis, we start from a population or sample of N statistical units, organized in a dataset where rows represent statistical units and columns represent observed variables. When analyzing two variables - denoted as X and F - at the same time, we build a bivariate distribution, represented via a contingency table (or crosstable), where:

  • each cell (i, j) contains the joint frequency nij, i.e. how many statistical units simultaneously exhibit xi and fi, or the relative joint frequency fij = nij/N.
  • at the borders of the crosstable, the row totals ni. = ∑jnij and column totals n.j = ∑inij recover the univariate distribution of X and F individually, called marginal distribution. Dividing by N gives the relative marginal frequencies fi. = ni./N and f.j = n.j/N.
Conditioning means restricting the reference total from the entire population to a specific subset defined by a modality of the other variable. The conditional relative frequency of xi given fi is defined as the ratio between the joint frequency and the corresponding marginal frequency: fi|j = nij/n.j = fij/f.j. Simmetrically, the conditional distribution of F given xi is fj|i = nij/ni. = fij/fi..

In descriptive statistics, we deal with known observed counts and distributions without uncertainty. Two statistical variables X and F are statistically independent if conditioning on one variable does not alter the distribution shape of the other variable. Formally, every conditional distribution must equal che corresponding marginal distribution: nij/n.j = ni./N for each i,j.
In terms of relative frequencies, independence holds when every joint relative frequency factorizes into the product of its marginal relative frequencies: fij = fi. · f.j. Since multiplication is commutative (fij = fji), independence is a symmetric property: if F has no influence on X, then X has no influence on F.

In probability theory, we deal with an unknown or infinite population. In this case, Kolmogorov's axiomatic probability space (Ω, F, P) provides the mathematical abstraction of empirical frequencies: relative frequencies correspond to probabilities (f → P).
Hence, properties that are obtained by counting in descriptive statistics are assumed as formal definitions in probability theory:

  • Two events A, B ∈ F are stochastically independent if the probability of their inteserction equals the product of their individual properties, i.e. P(A ⋂ B) = P(A) · P(B).
  • Conditioning does not change the probability of the event, i.e. P(A|B) = P(A ⋂ B)/P(B) = P(A), with P(B) > 0.

The link between the two domains is guaranteed by the Law of Large Numbers: as the sample size N → ∞, empirical relative frequencies fij converge to their theoretical probabilities P(X = xi ⋂ F = fi).

Chi-Square χ² and ρ-value

In a finite empirical sample of size N, random fluctuations prevent fij from equaling (fi. · f.j) exactly, even when the two underlying variables are independent.
Under the hypothesis of independence, the theoretical expected frequency Eij for cell (i, j) is achieved by scaling the product of the marginal relative frequencies by the sample size N: Eij = N · (fi. · f.j) = (ni. · n.j)/N. The achieved table of expected frequencies represents the counts we would observe in an idealized scenario of perfect independence while preserving the empirical row and colums totals.
To evaluate the discrepancy between the observed table Oij and the theoretical expected counts Eij across all cells, Pearson's Chi-square statistic is computed: $$\chi^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{R} \sum_{j=1}^{C} \frac{(O_{ij} - E_{ij})^2}{E_{ij}}$$ .
A value of χ² = 0 indicates exact empirical independence, while larger values signal stronger departures from it. Under the null hypothesis of independence, χ² asymptomatically follows a chi-square distribution with v = (R-1) · (C-1) degrees of freedom, i.e. the number of cells free to vary given fixed marginals.
From this distribution, we compute the ρ-value as P(χ²v ≥ χ²obs), which represents the probability of observing a discrepancy at least as extreme as χ²obs purely by chance. If ρ < α (where typically α = 0.05), we conclude that X and F are statistically dependent.

Observation

Even in an independent population (Case B), finite samples rarely yield χ² = 0. The ρ-value provides a formal threshold to distinguish random sampling variability from structural dependence.

Association vs. Causality

Although case A shows a strong statistical association between sport and faculty, statistical dependence (association) does not imply causation.
Enrolling in a faculty does not causally force a student to choose a sport over the others, nor does practicing a sport cause enrollment in a faculty.

Contingency tables and Chi-square statistic detect deviations from joint factorization, showing that conditioning on one variable alters the empirical distribution of the other, i.e. fi|j ≠ fi.. However, statistical tools measure only co-occurence within a dataset, not the underlying generative or physical mechanism linking the variables.
In general, an observed statistical dependency between two variables X and F can arise from 4 distinct scenarios:

  1. Symmetric Nature of Statistical Dependence vs. Asymmetric Causality - Statistical dependence is strictly symmetric, while causality is directional and asymmetric (X → F or F → X). A contingency table alone cannot distinguish whether X influences F, whether F influences X, or whether both mutually influence each other.
  2. Confounding Factors and Disturbing Variables - An apparent association between X and F is frequently induced by an unmeasured third variable Z (called confounder or disturbing factor), that simultaneously influences both X and F. If Z is omitted from the analysis, X and F appear strongly correlated even though no causal link exists between them.
  3. Sampling Bias and Lack of Representativeness - Statistical inference from a sample to a broader population is valid only if the sample is unbiased and representative with respect to the variables under study. If the data collection procedure introduces bias, artificial associations can emerge depending on how the dataset was constructed.
  4. Observational Analysis vs. Controlled Experiments - Passively observing a population through a survey reveals existing empirical association, while establishing a causal effect required a controlled experimental design. Techniques such as randomization, stratification, and blinded control groups are specifically designed to isolate causal relationships among the variables under study by breaking correlations with confounding variables.

Hence, while the χ² test provides a rigorous mathematical criterion to identify predictive relationships and structural dependencies in bivariate data, causal claims require domain knowledge, careful control and confounding variables, and dedicated experimental protocols rather than frequency analysis alone.
