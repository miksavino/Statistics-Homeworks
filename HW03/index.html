Adversarial Penetration Modeling: Bernoulli and Rademacher Processes

In an enterprise network composed of n distinct systems, the sequence of attack attemps executed by m independent adversaries is modeled as a discrete-time stochastic process.

Let the random variable Xi,j be the incremental penetration score achieved on the i-th system (i ∈ {1, 2, ..., n}) by the j-th adversary (j ∈ {1, 2, ..., m}).
It is assumed that for each adversary the increments {X1,j, X2,j, ..., Xn,j} are independent and identically distributed random variables drawn from a common probability distribution.
The cumulative score after testing k systems (k ≤ n) represents a random walk trajectory: \( S_{k,j} = \sum_{i=1}^{k} X_{i,j} \) .

The Bernoulli Model

In the Bernoulli Model, each increment variable is binary Xi ∈ {0, 1}, with probability mass function:

  • P(Xi = 1) = p, where Xi = 1 denotes a successful attack;
  • P(Xi = 0) = 1 - p, where Xi = 0 denotes an uncompromised system.
The cumulative score \( S_{n} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i} \) is the simple random walk with parameter p, which represents the total count of compromised systems, following a binomial distribution Binomial(n, p) with theoretical mean μ = 𝔼[Xi] = p and variance σ2 = Var(Xi) = p(1 - p).
Since all increments are non-negative, the penetration trajectory is monotonic non-decreasing (Sk+1 ≥ Sk), modeling an asymmetric environment where the defender canno patch or recover a system once it has been compromised.

The Rademacher Model

In the Rademacher Model, increments are symmetric and bipolar, taking values Xi ∈ {-1, 1} with equal probability P(Xi = 1) = P(Xi = -1) = 1/2, where Xi = 1 denotes an adversarial successfull attack, while Xi = -1 denotes an uncompromised system.
This model produces a classical one-dimensional random walk centered at zero, with a theoretical mean μ = 𝔼[Xi] = 0 and variance σ2 = Var(Xi) = 1. It acts as an equilibrium baseline where neither side has an inherent advantage. Hence, the model provides the ideal setting to analyze penetration trajectory, variance scaling and convergence toward the standard normal distribution via the Central Limit Theorem.

Asymptotic Analysis of Penetration Quantities

To understand how attack scores behave as the network size n grows, three distinct metrics are compared:

  • Total Score \( S_{n} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i} \) ;
  • Average Score \( S_{n}/n = (1/n)\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i} \) ;
  • Normalized Scaled Score \( S_{n}/√n = (1/√n)\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_{i} \) .
The difference among these metrics lies in how each factor affects the variance and the distribution profile as n → ∞.

Variance under Different Normalizations

Applying variance properties to sums of independent and identically distributed random variables yields three distinct asymptotic scaling regimes.

Since the increments are mutually independent, the covariance terms vanish (Cov(Xi, Xj) = 0 for i ≠ j), reducing the variance of their sum directly to the sum of their individual variances: \( \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \operatorname{Var}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{n} X_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sigma^2 = n\sigma^2 \) .
For the Total Score, the variance grows linearly with n. The resulting standard derivation SD(Sn) = √Var(Sn) = σ√n causes individual attacker trajectories to drift across a spacial interval of order 𝒪(√n), reflecting stochastic diffusion.

When examining the Average Score, factoring out the scalar constant 1/n squares the denominator: Var(Sn/n) = (1/n)2 Var(Sn) = 1/(n)2 · (nσ2) = σ2/n.
As n grows, the variance decays inversely to zero: \( \lim_{n \to \infty} \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{S_n}{n}\right) = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{\sigma^2}{n} = 0 \) .
This forces all trajectory averages to compress aroung the expected value 𝔼[Xi], ensuring deterministic stability.

For the Normalized Scalar Score, scaling by the factor 1/√n isolates the exact rate of disperion: \( \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} S_n\right) = \left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\right)^2 \operatorname{Var}(S_n) = \frac{1}{n} \cdot (n\sigma^2) = \sigma^2 \) .
The variance remains strictly invariant to the system size n.
Hence, the variance equals the single-step parameter across all scales: \( \operatorname{Var}\left(\frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}}\right) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{for the Rademacher model } (\sigma^2 = 1) \\ p(1-p) & \text{for the Bernoulli model } (\sigma^2 = p(1-p)) \end{cases} \) .

Convergence in Probability of Sn/n according to the Law of Large Numbers

According to the Law of Large Numbers, the average score converges in probability to the expected value μ as n increases: \( \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{S_n}{n}\right] = \mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n X_i\right] = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbb{E}[X_i] = \frac{1}{n} (n\mu) = \mu \implies \frac{S_n}{n} \xrightarrow{\mathbb{P}} \mu \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \) .
Evaluating this limit under the two models yields: \( \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{S_n}{n} = \begin{cases} p & \text{for the Bernoulli model } (\mu = p, \, \sigma^2 = p(1-p)) \\ 0 & \text{for the Rademacher model } (\mu = 0, \, \sigma^2 = 1) \end{cases} \) .

In cybersecurity terms, this proofs that stochastic operational noise cancels out across large infrastructures: the average compromise rate stabilized at the underlying system vulnerability parameter rather than fluctuating with the attacker luck.

Convergence in Distribution of Sn/√n according to the Central Limit Theorem

According to the Central Limit Theorem, the sums of independent increments converge toward a Gaussian distribution. Since the Rademacher model naturally has a zero mean and a unit variance, the Normalized Scaled Score converges to a standard normal distribution: \( \frac{S_n - np}{\sqrt{n p(1 - p)}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \) .
For large n values, plotting the outcomes of multiple attackers produces the Gaussial bell curve

In the Bernoulli model, since the mean is non-zero, the Normalized Scalar Score drifts upward by p√n.
Standardizing to \( \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \) requires centering around the mean: \( \frac{S_n - np}{\sqrt{n p(1 - p)}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1), \quad \frac{S_n}{\sqrt{n}} \xrightarrow{d} \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \quad \text{as } n \to \infty \) .

Cybersecurity Context

Role of Infrastructure Scale (n)

  • Total Successfull Attacks (Sn): in the Bernoulli model, the average number of compromised systems grows linearly as 𝔼[Sn] = np, while the spread increases at a slow rate of 𝒪(√n). Hence, larger networks will naturally suffer more total successfull attacks, even if every individual system has the same level of security.
  • Elimination of Luck (Sn/n): according to the Law of Large Numbers, the average breach rate gets closer to the vulnerability level p as n grows. Since the variance tends to zero, an attacker's luck vanishes over a large network. The final successfull attacks rate reflects the actual quality of the defenses rather than random chance.
  • Predictable Risk (Sn/√n): according to the Central Limit Theorem, the normalized scalar score follow a standard Gaussian bell curve. This allows security analysts to easily calculate worst-case probabilities (such as high-impact events) using a standard normal distribution, without having to calculate every individual combination by hand.

Role of Attacker Volume (m)

  • Testing the Full Attack Surface: testing just 1 attacker (m = 1) gives a single random result that can be very misleading. Testing how many attackers fill out the graph shows all possible outcomes, making the final histogram match the theoretical Gaussian bell curve.
  • Worst-Case vs. Average Risk: even if the average success rate is p, having many attackers means that some will get exponentially lucky and score higher than the average. In cybersecurity, defenders cannot only focus on the average attack, but they must build defenses strong enough to stop the worst-case attack found, which is the attack with maximum Sn.

Model Comparison: Bernoulli vs. Rademacher

  • Defensive Dynamics: in the Bernoulli model, breaches are permanent. In other words, once a system is compromised, it stays that way since there is no fix or recovery during the test. In the Rademacher model, there is a live contest between attack and defense since an attacker's win can be canceled out by a security patch or response.
  • Average Score: in the Bernoulli model, the average score moves toward the baseline success rate p. In the Rademacher model, the average score is 0, since wins and losses happen with equal chance.
  • Variance and Spread: in the Bernoulli model, the risk variance is controlled by p(1 - p), reaching its peak at p = 0.5 (σ2 = 0.25) and dropping close to 0 if defenses are either nearly impenetrable (low p) or completely broken (high p). In the Rademacher model, every step has a fixed variance of 1, leading to a wider spread of trajectories over time.
